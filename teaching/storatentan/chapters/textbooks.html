Appendix C — Textbooks and general resources

This appendix lists the textbooks that cover the exam topics, the resources that help you keep up with a fast-moving field, and a set of classical papers that shaped it.

C.1 Textbooks

No single textbook is required. The books below cover most of the material, but none is fully up to date, so use them for the foundations and the papers in the chapters for recent developments.

  • Zvelebil and Baum (2008) — Understanding Bioinformatics. Broad coverage; recommended in the exam description.
  • Pevsner (2015) — Bioinformatics and Functional Genomics. Broad coverage; the basis of the 2021 exam variant.
  • Jones and Pevzner (2004) — An Introduction to Bioinformatics Algorithms. Algorithms: alignment, suffix trees, assembly, phylogeny and motifs.
  • Compeau and Pevzner (2018) — Bioinformatics Algorithms: An Active Learning Approach. With free online lectures.
  • Durbin et al. (1998) — Biological Sequence Analysis. The classic on probabilistic models of sequences, including pairwise alignment, profile HMMs and phylogeny.
  • Felsenstein (2004) — Inferring Phylogenies. The standard reference on phylogenetic methods.
  • Burkowski (2008) — Structural Bioinformatics: An Algorithmic Approach. Structure, dynamic programming, RNA secondary structure, superposition and machine learning.
  • Gagniuc (2021) — Algorithms in Bioinformatics: Theory and Implementation. Alignment, motifs and Markov chains.
  • Hastie et al. (2009) — The Elements of Statistical Learning. The standard reference on statistical machine learning.

C.2 Keeping up to date

The field changes faster than any textbook. These sources show what is new:

  • the proceedings of the most recent ISMB and ECCB conferences (published as supplements of Bioinformatics);
  • the annual Nucleic Acids Research Database and Web Server issues;
  • seminars and dissertations at DBB and SciLifeLab.

C.3 Collections of papers

C.3.1 Classical papers in bioinformatics

These papers were read in the PhD course Classical papers in bioinformatics (SciLifeLab, Arne Elofsson and Måns Magnusson). Each made a fundamental contribution or changed how the field thinks. For each paper, ask: what was new about it, how did it change the field, and what has happened in the field since? They are grouped in the order of the chapters.

Sequencing and sequence search

  • Sanger et al. (1977) — DNA sequencing with chain-terminating inhibitors (Section 5.2).
  • Li and Durbin (2009) — BWA: short-read alignment with the Burrows–Wheeler transform (Section 1.4, Section 5.6).

Phylogeny

  • Fitch (1971) — parsimony: minimum change on a given tree (Section 4.3).
  • Felsenstein (1981) — maximum-likelihood trees from DNA sequences (Section 4.3).
  • Huelsenbeck et al. (2001) — Bayesian inference of phylogeny (Section 4.3).

Protein structure and folding

  • Levinthal (1966) — molecular model building by computer; the paradox itself (Section 9.4) is from Levinthal (1968).
  • Chou and Fasman (1974a); Chou and Fasman (1974b) — the Chou–Fasman secondary-structure propensities and prediction method (Section 9.8).
  • Chothia et al. (1977) — how α-helices and β-sheets pack against each other (Section 9.3).
  • McCammon et al. (1977) — the first molecular dynamics simulation of a protein (Section 9.11).
  • Meng et al. (1992) — DOCK with grid-based energy evaluation (Section 9.11); the original DOCK is Kuntz et al. (1982).
  • Dalal et al. (1997) — “protein alchemy”: changing a β-sheet protein into an α-helical one by changing half of its sequence.
  • Bradley et al. (2005) — Rosetta reaches high-resolution de novo structure prediction for small proteins (Section 9.8).

Membrane proteins and signal peptides

  • Heijne (1986) — weight-matrix prediction of signal-peptide cleavage sites (Section 10.5).
  • Heijne (1992) — hydrophobicity analysis and the positive-inside rule (Section 9.10).
  • Krogh et al. (2001) — TMHMM: a hidden Markov model for transmembrane topology (Section 1.6.4, Section 9.10).
  • Nielsen et al. (1997) — SignalP: neural networks for signal peptides (Section 10.5).
